Let me just come right out and say it: _working weekends is not something we should be proud of_.

Work-life balance gets something of a bad rap in startup culture these days. I don’t think it’s deserved, and in many ways is actually counterproductive.

The narrative sounds something like this:

* “Don’t expect to be the best if you want to have work-life balance.”
* “If you’re under 30, the greatest advantage you have is working longer hours than the guy next to you.”
* “If you’re tired, do more.”
* “Weekends are for building, right?”

And whether it's actually said or merely implied by the prevailing mood, the result is the same.

I personally know what lies on the other side of 80 hour weeks from experience. I have tasted the Kool-Aid myself: burnout, exhaustion, sleep deprivation, chronic bags drooping under bloodshot eyes…

I struggled for years to free myself from this pernicious influence, and it's taken me much experience to overcome—learning to trust my own judgment on the matter, taking a critical eye to the collective habit. So what I hope I can offer is an alternative perspective that is more appropriate for creative work, and which is almost certainly better adapted to a world in which the actual execution of our work is done increasingly by AI.

#### work is a product of vectors

I’ve always loved physics because it has a way of clarifying things. In physics, work is not just effort—it is the result of force applied along a direction of motion. Work arises from the product of two _vectors_: the force we apply, and the displacement that results. Without direction, the force does nothing. With the wrong direction, the force actually _undoes_ progress. Only when aligned do force and motion accumulate as positive work.

[ Formula ]

Mathematically, work is the integral of force along a path. Both force and displacement are vectors. The work done is given by the dot product of the two vectors, where the result is a scalar. You can imagine it as the area under a curve—the sum of all those tiny steps where force and motion line up.

It is therefore not enough to ask _how much_ you’ve worked. You must also ask: _in which direction_?

And here lies the indispensable corollary: there is such a thing as _Negative Work_.
